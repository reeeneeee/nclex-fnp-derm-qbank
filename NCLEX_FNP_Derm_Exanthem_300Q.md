# 300 Hard NCLEX/FNP Questions — Pediatric Skin & Viral Exanthems

**Q1. [SATA]** A family nurse practitioner (FNP) is deciding between topical and systemic therapy for children with KOH-confirmed tinea corporis seen in clinic this week. Which findings support systemic therapy rather than topical therapy alone? Select all that apply.
A. A 9-year-old with annular scaling plaques on the cheek and chin extending to the jawline
B. A 10-year-old with trunk lesions and a thickened, dystrophic great toenail with subungual debris
C. A 6-year-old with three 2-cm forearm lesions partially improved after 7 days of twice-daily clotrimazole
D. A 7-year-old with trunk lesions who is receiving maintenance chemotherapy for leukemia
E. An 8-year-old with a single thigh lesion with superficial pustules along its border
F. A 5-year-old with two trunk lesions that have spread peripherally and cleared centrally over 2 weeks

**Answer:** A, B, D
**Rationale:** Tinea faciei (A), a tinea infection of the nails at the same time (B), and immunosuppression (D) are all listed indications for systemic therapy, as are extensive infection and failure of topical treatment. Option C is not a topical failure: lesions are expected to take 1 to 4 weeks to resolve, and partial improvement at 1 week is on track. Superficial pustules (E) can be part of an inflammatory tinea corporis lesion and do not by themselves call for systemic therapy. Peripheral spread with central clearing (F) is the classic localized picture and is treated topically.

**Q2. [Single]** A 7-year-old with extensive tinea corporis and tinea of the nails at the same time is starting oral griseofulvin, and an extended course is expected. Which laboratory monitoring plan matches the recommended schedule?
A. CBC, liver, and renal function 4 weeks after starting therapy and every 4 weeks until it is stopped
B. CBC, liver, and renal function 8 weeks after starting therapy and every 8 weeks until it is stopped
C. CBC, liver, and renal function 8 weeks after starting therapy and once more when therapy is completed
D. CBC, liver, and renal function 2 weeks after starting therapy and every 8 weeks until it is stopped

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** Griseofulvin carries a risk of hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and neutropenia. Patients on extended therapy should have a CBC, liver function, and renal function checked 8 weeks after starting and every 8 weeks until treatment stops. The 4-week interval (A) and the 2-week first check (D) do not match this schedule. Option C drops the repeated every-8-week checks needed for the rest of the course.

**Q3. [SATA]** A 6-year-old with atopic dermatitis has an annular, scaly plaque on the trunk that was mistaken for an eczema flare and treated with several different creams. Which topical agents, applied to a dermatophyte infection, can produce tinea incognito? Select all that apply.
A. Tacrolimus 0.03% ointment used alone
B. Pimecrolimus 1% cream used alone
C. Triamcinolone 0.1% cream used alone
D. Clotrimazole-betamethasone combination cream
E. Terbinafine 1% cream used alone
F. Ciclopirox 0.77% cream used alone

**Answer:** A, B, C, D
**Rationale:** Tinea incognito is a dermatophyte infection altered by topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) or steroid creams, used alone or together with a topical antifungal. That is why the steroid-antifungal combination (D) also qualifies. An antifungal used alone, whether an allylamine (E) or ciclopirox (F), treats the infection and does not hide it, so neither causes tinea incognito.

**Q4. [Single]** A 5-year-old who attends day care has a KOH-confirmed tinea corporis lesion on the forearm. The parent will apply the first dose of topical clotrimazole tonight at 7 PM. When may the child return to day care?
A. After 48 hours of antifungal treatment have elapsed
B. After 72 hours of antifungal treatment have elapsed
C. After 24 hours of antifungal treatment have elapsed
D. After the forearm lesion has completely cleared

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** Children with tinea corporis are excluded from day care or school until 24 hours after treatment has begun. The 48-hour and 72-hour options (A, B) exclude the child longer than recommended. Waiting for complete clearing (D) is unnecessary, since topical clearing can take 1 to 4 weeks.

**Q5. [SATA]** A 16-year-old obese male has had an itchy groin rash for 3 weeks. Which examination findings are consistent with tinea cruris? Select all that apply.
A. Erythematous to slightly brown plaques with sharp margins on the inner thighs
B. A raised advancing border containing scale, pustules, or vesicles
C. Partial central clearing within the plaques
D. Satellite papulopustules scattered beyond the main plaque border
E. Beefy-red, moist plaques with no defined advancing border
F. Involvement of the left thigh only, which rules out a dermatophyte

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** Tinea cruris appears as erythematous to slightly brown, sharply marginated plaques with a raised border of scale, pustules, or vesicles, and central clearing may be present. Satellite papulopustules (D) and beefy-red plaques with no defined border (E) point to candidiasis or intertrigo instead. Tinea cruris is usually bilateral and symmetric but not always, so one-sided involvement (F) does not rule it out.

**Q6. [Single]** A 15-year-old has KOH-confirmed tinea pedis between the fourth and fifth toes. Which instruction for the topical antifungal is correct?
A. Apply it 1 cm beyond the rash border twice daily until 7 days after clearing
B. Apply it 2 cm beyond the rash border twice daily until 14 days after clearing
C. Apply it only to the visible rash twice daily until 7 days after clearing
D. Apply it 1 cm beyond the rash border once daily until 14 days after clearing

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** For tinea pedis, the antifungal is applied 1 cm beyond the borders of the rash twice daily and continued until 7 days after clearing; the usual course is 3 to 6 weeks. Option B has the wrong margin and the wrong end point. Option D has the wrong frequency and the wrong end point. Option C leaves out the margin of normal-appearing skin beyond the border, where fungus is still present.

**Q7. [SATA]** An 8-year-old is on day 10 of topical clotrimazole for tinea corporis on the arm. The child now has a new, itchy papulovesicular eruption on the trunk, hands, and face. Which statements about this eruption are accurate? Select all that apply.
A. It is a hypersensitivity response to the fungus
B. Its spread to the trunk, hands, and face is typical of this reaction
C. KOH of the new papulovesicles is expected to show many hyphae
D. It shows an allergy to clotrimazole, so all azoles must be avoided permanently
E. It is called an id response
F. It confirms tinea incognito caused by the topical antifungal

**Answer:** A, B, E
**Rationale:** An id response is an itchy papulovesicular rash on the trunk, hands, or face caused by a hypersensitivity response to the fungus. The id lesions themselves do not contain fungus, so KOH of them is expected to be negative (C). The reaction is to the fungus, not to the drug (D). Tinea incognito (F) is caused by steroids or calcineurin inhibitors, not by an antifungal used alone.

**Q12. [Single]** A 9-year-old returns 2 weeks after starting twice-daily clotrimazole for a presumed tinea corporis lesion on the shoulder. The lesion is unchanged. What is the most appropriate next step?
A. Add hydrocortisone 1% to the clotrimazole and reassess in 2 weeks
B. Obtain a fungal culture and switch to a different class of antifungal
C. Continue clotrimazole for 2 more weeks before obtaining a fungal culture
D. Obtain a fungal culture and switch to a different azole such as miconazole

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** Follow-up is at 2 weeks. If the lesion has not responded, either the diagnosis is wrong or the fungus may be resistant, so obtain a culture to confirm the diagnosis and change to a different class of antifungal. Miconazole (D) is an azole like clotrimazole, so it is not a change of class. Continuing the same drug (C) delays the workup. Adding a steroid (A) risks tinea incognito and goes against the advice to avoid steroid-antifungal combinations.

The main fetal risk is anemia leading to hydrops fetalis, so the fetus is monitored with serial ultrasounds and middle cerebral artery Doppler for about 8 to 12 weeks. If severe anemia is found, intrauterine transfusion can be done. B19 is not linked to a congenital malformation syndrome (B). Maternal IVIG has no proven role (D). The risk is highest in the first half of pregnancy, so 14 weeks is not a low-risk time (C).

**Q289. [SATA]** A 28-year-old at 12 weeks of gestation has serologically confirmed acute parvovirus B19 infection. Which counseling statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A. Parvovirus B19 is not an established cause of a congenital malformation syndrome
B. The main fetal risk is anemia leading to hydrops, and the risk is greatest when infection occurs in the first half of pregnancy
C. Most pregnancies complicated by maternal B19 infection end with a healthy infant
D. Pregnant school staff should be routinely excluded from work during community outbreaks
E. Severe fetal anemia identified on monitoring may be treated with intrauterine transfusion
F. A woman who was IgG-positive and IgM-negative before the exposure is susceptible to infection

**Answer:** A, B, C, E
**Rationale:** B19 crosses the placenta and infects fetal red cell precursors. The result can be anemia, hydrops fetalis, or fetal death, with the greatest risk in the first half of pregnancy. It is not a known teratogen, and most infected pregnancies have good outcomes. Fetal anemia can be treated with intrauterine transfusion. Routine exclusion of pregnant workers is not recommended because spread is widespread before the rash. Positive IgG with negative IgM before exposure means prior immunity.

**Q290. [Single]** A 34-year-old mother of a child recently diagnosed with fifth disease has had painful, swollen hands, wrists, and knees on both sides for 5 days. She has no rash and no tick exposure. Which plan is most appropriate?

**Answer:** B

**Q291. [SATA]** An NP is teaching parents how parvovirus B19 spreads. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.

E. It spreads mainly by the fecal-oral route in child care settings

**Answer:** A, B, C, F

**Q292. [Single]** A 6-year-old developed slapped cheeks and a lacy rash this week. His classmate's pregnant mother asks when he was most likely contagious. Which answer is correct?
A. During flares of the rash brought on by sun exposure in the following weeks
B. During the slapped-cheek phase, when the facial redness was most intense
C. During the lacy rash phase on the trunk and the extensor surfaces of the arms and legs
D. During the mild, nonspecific illness in the days before the facial rash appeared

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:** In erythema infectiosum, communicability is highest before the rash appears, during the mild prodrome of low-grade fever, myalgia, headache, and URI symptoms. By the time the slapped-cheek and lacy rash phases appear, the immunocompetent child is no longer contagious. Recurrent flares with heat or sunlight are not a sign of renewed contagiousness.

**Q293. [SATA]** A 6-year-old with acute lymphoblastic leukemia on maintenance chemotherapy has persistent anemia with reticulocytopenia, and chronic parvovirus B19 infection is suspected. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.

**Answer:** A, B, C

**Q297. [SATA]** An NP is explaining the typical course of erythema infectiosum to a family whose child was exposed at school. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.

B. Only a minority of children (about 15% to 30%) have mild fever during the prodrome

E. The lacy rash goes away for good within 48 hours, with no recurrence
F. The rash usually appears 2 to 3 days after the prodrome begins

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** The incubation period is about 4 to 21 days, and the rash and symptoms occur about 2 to 3 weeks after exposure. Mild fever occurs in only 15% to 30% of patients during the prodrome. **Q298. [Single]** A 15-year-old has a low-grade fever, small mouth erosions, and painful, itchy swelling with petechiae and purpura on both hands and feet. The lesions stop sharply at the wrists and ankles. Which virus is the most likely cause?

B. Human herpesvirus 6
C. Rubella virus

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:** Papular-purpuric "gloves and socks" syndrome is a well-recognized presentation of parvovirus B19, usually in adolescents and young adults. **Q299. [SATA]** A hospital infection preventionist asks the NP about isolation for patients with parvovirus B19. Which recommendations are correct? Select all that apply.

**Q300. [Single]** Four 6-year-olds in the same classroom were exposed to a classmate who developed fifth disease. For which child is the exposure most concerning for transient aplastic crisis?
A. A child with sickle cell disease (HbSS) and a baseline reticulocyte count of 12%
B. A child whose iron deficiency anemia resolved with 3 months of oral iron
C. A child who is a silent carrier of alpha-thalassemia with normal red cell indices
D. A child with sickle cell trait (HbAS) and a normal baseline hemoglobin

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** Transient aplastic crisis happens when B19 halts red cell production in a patient whose red cells already have a shortened lifespan, as in sickle cell disease or hereditary spherocytosis. That is why a high baseline reticulocyte count signals vulnerability. Sickle cell trait, silent alpha-thalassemia carrier status, and resolved iron deficiency do not involve chronic hemolysis, so a short pause in red cell production is not clinically significant in those children.
