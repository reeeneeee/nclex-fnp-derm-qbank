**Q1. [SATA]** A family nurse practitioner (FNP) is deciding between topical and systemic therapy for children with KOH-confirmed tinea corporis seen in clinic this week. Which findings support systemic therapy rather than topical therapy alone? Select all that apply.
A. A 9-year-old with annular scaling plaques on the cheek and chin extending to the jawline
B. A 10-year-old with trunk lesions and a thickened, dystrophic great toenail with subungual debris
C. A 6-year-old with three 2-cm forearm lesions partially improved after 7 days of twice-daily clotrimazole
D. A 7-year-old with trunk lesions who is receiving maintenance chemotherapy for leukemia
E. An 8-year-old with a single thigh lesion with superficial pustules along its border
F. A 5-year-old with two trunk lesions that have spread peripherally and cleared centrally over 2 weeks

**Answer:** A, B, D
**Rationale:** Tinea faciei (A), a tinea infection of the nails at the same time (B), and immunosuppression (D) are all listed indications for systemic therapy, as are extensive infection and failure of topical treatment. Option C is not a topical failure: lesions are expected to take 1 to 4 weeks to resolve, and partial improvement at 1 week is on track. Superficial pustules (E) can be part of an inflammatory tinea corporis lesion and do not by themselves call for systemic therapy. Peripheral spread with central clearing (F) is the classic localized picture and is treated topically.

**Q2. [Single]** A 7-year-old with extensive tinea corporis and tinea of the nails at the same time is starting oral griseofulvin, and an extended course is expected. Which laboratory monitoring plan matches the recommended schedule?

B. CBC, liver, and renal function 8 weeks after starting therapy and every 8 weeks until it is stopped
C. CBC, liver, and renal function 8 weeks after starting therapy and once more when therapy is completed
D. CBC, liver, and renal function 2 weeks after starting therapy and every 8 weeks until it is stopped

**Answer:** B

**Q3. [SATA]** A 6-year-old with atopic dermatitis has an annular, scaly plaque on the trunk that was mistaken for an eczema flare and treated with several different creams. Which topical agents, applied to a dermatophyte infection, can produce tinea incognito? Select all that apply.
A. Tacrolimus 0.03% ointment used alone
B. Pimecrolimus 1% cream used alone
C. Triamcinolone 0.1% cream used alone
D. Clotrimazole-betamethasone combination cream
E. Terbinafine 1% cream used alone
F. Ciclopirox 0.77% cream used alone

**Answer:** A, B, C, D
**Rationale:** Tinea incognito is a dermatophyte infection altered by topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) or steroid creams, used alone or together with a topical antifungal. An antifungal used alone, whether an allylamine (E) or ciclopirox (F), treats the infection and does not hide it, so neither causes tinea incognito.
